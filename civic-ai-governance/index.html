Civic AI Governance

Canonical research branch · 17 September 2026

Core question

Frontier AI companies and prominent technology leaders increasingly describe advanced AI as carrying potentially severe societal or catastrophic risks. Those warnings may reflect genuine safety concerns, commercial incentives, regulatory strategy, institutional responsibility, or combinations of these factors. Omoluabi will not assume the answer.

When frontier-AI companies describe AI as extraordinarily powerful and potentially dangerous, how does that rhetoric interact with their commercial interests, regulation, market concentration and control of compute?

The proposition that AI-risk rhetoric can also function as marketing is treated as a testable hypothesis, not a conclusion.

Investigation frame

  • What companies and executives publicly say about AI risk.
  • What systems they release and on what timetable.
  • How rapidly they scale compute.
  • What regulation they support, oppose or seek to shape.
  • How safety commitments change over time.
  • How much independent evaluation and oversight they permit.
  • Who can access frontier compute.
  • Where infrastructure and capital expenditure are concentrated.

The African governance question

The branch will not begin with “How should Africa regulate American AI?” It begins with:

What institutions should govern African AI infrastructure that Africans themselves own, operate or substantially control?

This opens research into public compute, community ownership, African-language models, local data trusts, mineral provenance, energy rights, accessibility, model transparency, procurement, research access, environmental accounting and citizen appeal.

Compute Sovereignty

ENERGY + MATERIALS + HARDWARE + COMPUTE + DATA + MODELS + GOVERNANCE

A country can possess critical minerals while retaining little control over processing or high-value manufacturing. It can possess data while renting the compute. It can deploy local-language models while depending on externally controlled infrastructure. Compute Sovereignty asks where meaningful agency must exist across the stack.

Governance research areas

1. Frontier-AI alarm

Create a dated evidence base of public risk statements, safety frameworks and policy positions from major AI companies and technology leaders.

2. Safety vs. commercial incentives

Compare stated safety positions with releases, investment, compute expansion, lobbying, partnerships and market strategy.

3. Compute concentration

Map ownership and control of chips, hyperscale cloud, data centers, model infrastructure and access.

4. African governance frameworks

Compare the African Union Continental AI Strategy, national strategies, data-protection regimes, procurement rules and regional institutions.

5. Mineral and compute sovereignty

Trace the relationship between resource extraction, processing, component production, hardware, cloud infrastructure and model access.

6. Public-interest AI

Investigate public, university, cooperative, municipal, nonprofit and commons-based compute models.

7. Rights and accountability

Develop proposals around accessibility, due process, auditability, environmental accounting, public procurement, community consent and redress.

Method

Omoluabi will explicitly distinguish fact, attributed claim, hypothesis, inference and unknown. Contradictions remain visible rather than being flattened into a predetermined narrative.

Relationship to AEN

AEN asks how African-controlled computational infrastructure could be physically designed, powered and built. Civic AI Governance asks who should own it, how it should be governed, what rights people retain, and what public obligations accompany control of compute.

AEN builds the physical possibility. Omoluabi investigates the civic conditions.
