# Omọlúwabí Investigates: Iowa Army Lead Azide Detonation

**Research cut-off:** November 19, 2026  
**Status:** Developing event; detonation is scheduled for November 20, 2026  
**Question:** What is happening at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant, why is the Army planning a detonation, what does the lead-plume warning mean, and what remains unverified?

## Finding

This is a real, planned Army operation at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP), outside Middletown. The Army plans two controlled detonations of 68 drums of degraded lead azide, an explosive compound containing lead. The EPA permit redacts the exact quantity; public reporting puts it at roughly 9,700 pounds.

The Army says the material recrystallized and became too sensitive to move or handle. It chose detonation in place as the least dangerous available disposal route. The choice trades an uncontrolled, hard-to-predict explosion risk for a planned blast that can be contained and monitored—but the planned event itself is expected to release some lead-containing particles. The official maps are conservative modeled impact areas, not measurements of actual contamination and not proof that every shaded location will experience a harmful dose.

The Army has asked residents in the Middletown City Park area to leave temporarily from 8 a.m. to 2 p.m. Central Time on November 20, primarily because vibration could break windows. This is not a request for all of Iowa—or all of Middletown—to relocate. People in broader areas on the Army's maps receive exposure precautions rather than the same evacuation request.

**Assessment:** The core explosive hazard and the planned operation are well documented. The Army's rationale is plausible and supported by the EPA permit record, but the public cannot yet independently evaluate the risk trade-off completely. Important questions remain about the condition and history of the drums, the model-to-health interpretation, notice to communities outside Middletown, final NEPA decision status, and the reach and public release of post-detonation sampling.

## What the material is—and why the Army says it must act

Lead azide is a primary explosive used in detonators. According to the Army's Environmental Assessment (EA), a routine safety review found the degraded material in March 2026. Temperature changes in its ethanol/water storage solution allowed material to dissolve and recrystallize as sensitive crystals. Explosive Ordnance Disposal (EOD) personnel then advised that the drums not be moved because physical handling, packing, transport, or burial could cause an accidental detonation.

The EPA issued emergency permits first for storage and then for in-place treatment. Its November 13 treatment permit describes the material as both reactive hazardous waste (D003) and toxic for lead (D008). EPA says the instability created an imminent and substantial endangerment and says the Army's alternatives review resulted in selection of in-place detonation.

The Army's alternatives analysis, summarized in its November 18 EA and local reporting, considered:

| Option | Why the Army rejected or selected it |
|---|---|
| Leave it in storage | The Army says instability could worsen and an accidental detonation would be uncontrolled; storage also remains subject to hazardous-waste requirements. |
| Move drums to another on-site detonation unit or off site | EOD judged handling and transport too dangerous. |
| Chemically neutralize the material | The EA describes a 7–22 day process requiring repeated hands-on work with the drums; the Army judged the handling risk unacceptable. |
| Detonate without containment and dust controls | Rejected because of the risk of spreading lead dust. |
| Controlled detonation in place | Selected: leave drums in their earth-covered magazines, use blast barriers and water suppression, choose weather conditions, model the plume, monitor, and clean up residues. |

This is the Army's and EPA's stated risk comparison, not an independently reproduced explosives-safety analysis. The key evidence supporting the urgency is the EOD no-move directive and the EPA permit record. The underlying technical decision files and some facility/location details are not all in the public permit; the permit says the administrative record will be made available separately.

## What the maps actually show

The Army's public package contains two different model products:

1. **Air-dispersion contour:** where modeled airborne lead could be present, using an outer concentration of 0.18 µg/m³. EPA's permit says the model must use an emission factor of **1.00**—a deliberately conservative assumption that treats all lead as emitted for notification modeling. EPA explicitly says the model may not represent actual conditions and that the Army expects actual airborne release to be lower, but the agencies could not quantify that reduction reliably in advance.
2. **Surface-deposition contours:** predicted deposition bands at 5 µg/ft² and 100 µg/ft². Under the permit, the higher band triggers advice to wet-clean high-contact surfaces, in addition to staying indoors and other exposure precautions.

These shaded areas are not a live sensor map, an observed spill boundary, or a forecast that everyone inside will receive the contour's exact concentration. They are planning and notification contours generated from a conservative model. The map boundary is not itself a safe/unsafe line. The Army says weather changes can shift the plume; it must post an updated map six hours before the event and hourly after that until the operation is complete, then provide a final estimate within six hours after completion.

The EA also summarizes a separate IMAAC worst-case air analysis: it says concentrations could exceed PAC-1 (temporary, reversible discomfort) but not PAC-2 or PAC-3. Those are acute protective-action criteria. The public record does not clearly reconcile this model with the EPA-required lead-plume maps, which use a separate conservative lead emission factor and are expressly meant to locate potential exposure areas. They should not be treated as interchangeable analyses.

## Safety steps supported by current official guidance

This is an information guide, not an emergency order. People should use the newest Army map and follow local public-health or emergency-management direction.

- **If you are in the identified Middletown City Park evacuation area:** leave between 8 a.m. and 2 p.m. Central Time on November 20 as the Army asks. The updated Army map package lists the Armed Forces Reserve Center, 16815 DMC Highway 34, Middletown, as a temporary place to go for identified residents; access is controlled. Confirm with the Army if you need help.
- **If your home or workplace is within the latest modeled area:** follow the Army/EPA notice for the specific exposure period. The EPA permit's public instructions say to stay indoors if you cannot leave, close windows and exterior doors, set HVAC to recirculate/minimize outside air if safe, and avoid travel through the affected area.
- **For Illinois residents who may be affected:** Illinois Department of Public Health advises staying indoors with windows closed for 24–48 hours after the detonation; it also advises handwashing, washing outdoor surfaces with soap and water, and removing shoes at the door to limit tracking dust indoors. This is state guidance; Iowa residents should follow Iowa/local incident-specific instructions.
- **After the Army's all-clear:** use the current map and official cleanup directions. EPA's permit calls for wet-cleaning high-contact surfaces in the higher modeled deposition area and requires sampling at qualifying parks/public facilities and private properties where the Army has permission to enter. Do not dry-sweep suspected dust.
- **If you have a health concern or think a child may have been exposed:** contact a clinician or local public-health department and ask whether blood-lead testing is appropriate. Illinois IDPH specifically recommends discussing a blood test when exposure is a concern.
- **If someone is in immediate danger:** contact local emergency services. For operation-specific updates, use the Army's IAAAP public-notifications page and local emergency-management/public-health sources.

## Public-trust and evidence gaps

### 1. The public first saw the regional plume only days before the planned operation

The Army held an October 15 open house, but its earlier community FAQ did not disclose a regional plume map or describe the broad potential map area. Iowa Starting Line reports that the first plume map was released November 15, and quotes the Army spokesperson acknowledging that the map was new information to people outside Middletown. The EPA permit requires a modeled notification 48 hours before the event; that requirement is a minimum timing rule, not proof that earlier public outreach was adequate.

### 2. The official FONSI copy is unsigned in the public record reviewed

The Army's November 18 FONSI PDF has blank commander signature and date lines and says the commander is recommended to approve and sign it. The Army-hosted publication page confirms it was posted November 18, but the public copy reviewed here does not establish whether it was subsequently signed. This is a document-status question, not by itself proof that the detonation is unauthorized. The EPA RCRA permit authorizes hazardous-waste treatment; it is not the same document as the Army's NEPA decision.

### 3. The map is conservative, but actual release and exposure are still unknown

EPA requires an emission factor of 1.00 because it could not quantify the reduction from containment and suppression. The Army believes the real release will be lower. Actual airborne lead, where dust settles, and whether property cleanup is required will only be known after event monitoring and sampling. The Army should publish raw and validated results, sampling locations, lab methods, detection limits, and the final modeled footprint promptly.

### 4. Cross-state coordination has been criticized

Illinois EPA and Illinois Department of Public Health issued their own guidance on November 18, said Mercer County could be affected under then-current modeling, and said Illinois EPA had not been notified by EPA Region 7. Illinois asked for comprehensive, public air, soil, and surface-dust sampling. This is a documented state-agency concern, not proof that Illinois is outside the federal permit's coverage; the EPA permit requires corrective action beyond the installation boundary where needed to protect health and the environment.

### 5. The public materials need a clearer bridge between technical criteria and household choices

The Army says the plume may include lead particles, while the EA describes a short-term and localized effect and compares the modeled air result with PAC thresholds. EPA's permit separately mandates broad, conservative lead contours for public notices. Residents need one plain-language explanation connecting: map contour → which actions apply → exact start/end time → who determines all-clear → what to do if sampling detects lead. Current materials leave a gap here.

## Questions residents and local officials should ask now

1. Is the commander-signed FONSI available, and what is the final go/no-go authority?
2. Can the Army publish the latest map package in a machine-readable format and state what map changes mean for each affected town?
3. What quantity of lead and net explosive weight is present, and why are those figures redacted?
4. What precise instruments, locations, sampling intervals, and laboratory methods will be used before, during, and after detonation—including in Illinois?
5. Who receives immediate notice of a monitor result above the applicable threshold, who issues all-clear, and how will residents receive it?
6. Will the Army offer voluntary residential sampling to all potentially affected residents, including people who do not fall in the highest deposition band?
7. When will validated data, maps, sample locations, cleanup decisions, and a plain-language after-action report be public?
