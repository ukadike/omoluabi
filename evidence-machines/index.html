Evidence Machines: Investigating the Unexplained

A studio about how to investigate difficult public evidence with code — without turning uncertainty into spectacle.

Students work across archives, testimony, audio, video, images, maps, metadata, APIs, transcripts, structured data, AI-assisted research, and visualization. The UAP/UFO archive is the first stress-test corpus because it makes provenance, contradiction, missingness, institutional uncertainty, and public interpretation unusually visible.

The subject is not “Are UFOs real?” The subject is: what does the evidence actually permit us to say?

COURSE PROPOSAL

A seven-week computational investigation studio

FORMAT7-week graduate studio / seminar
MODEResearch + making + critique
FINALPublic evidence machine
CODEBeginner-accessible, project-driven

Evidence Machines asks students to investigate phenomena that are hard to read because the evidence is incomplete, multimodal, politically or culturally charged, technically mediated, or distributed across incompatible archives. Students do not begin by proving a theory. They begin by building a traceable evidence environment.

The studio combines computational media, investigative journalism, archival practice, critical AI, data visualization, interface design, and accessibility. Each student chooses a contested or difficult case and develops it from claim → source → structured evidence → contradiction → visualization → public interface.

Why this belongs at ITP / IMA

Computation as a way of seeing

Students use code to expose relationships that prose alone can hide: temporal gaps, source inheritance, contradictory accounts, missing modalities, and changes in a claim over time.

The weird as a rigorous research condition

Strange material forces students to confront uncertainty, ambiguity, institutional authority, belief, and the limits of their tools without rewarding sensationalism.

AI as instrument, not oracle

Students can use language and multimodal models for search assistance, extraction, transcription, comparison, and interface prototyping, but machine output must remain distinguishable from source evidence.

Accessibility as epistemology

A record that cannot be perceived or navigated by part of the public is an incomplete public record. Access therefore shapes how evidence is represented from the beginning.

Learning outcomes

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

  1. Trace a claim backward from circulation to the strongest retrievable primary or near-primary source.
  2. Structure evidence as data using JSON, CSV, tables, schemas, identifiers, dates, source classes, and uncertainty states.
  3. Use code to interrogate evidence rather than merely decorate a finished argument.
  4. Separate observation from interpretation and later recollection from contemporaneous record.
  5. Build visualizations that preserve uncertainty instead of manufacturing false precision.
  6. Publish an accessible, inspectable investigation with citations, transcripts, alt text, source data, and a reproducible reasoning trail.

Seven weeks

WEEK 01 · THE CLAIM

What exactly are you investigating?

Separate question, claim, assumption, and desired conclusion. Build the first claim ledger.

Lab: HTML + semantic evidence card.

Deliverable: one-page investigation brief and evidence threshold.

WEEK 02 · FIND THE ORIGINAL

Where did this come from?

Government archives, institutional repositories, oral history, scientific literature, APIs, FOIA, metadata, and source inheritance.

Lab: JSON / CSV source ledger; basic Python or JavaScript parsing.

Deliverable: source ladder with at least one primary-source retrieval.

WEEK 03 · EVIDENCE HAS A BODY

What can this medium actually record?

Audio, video, photography, sensor systems, maps, transcripts, compression, metadata, and the difference between signal and representation.

Lab: media metadata + synchronized transcript or image annotation.

Deliverable: modality audit: what is present, absent, and unknowable.

WEEK 04 · MACHINES AS INVESTIGATIVE PARTNERS

What should AI be allowed to do?

Search support, extraction, transcription, clustering, comparison, agent workflows, hallucination, provenance leakage, and human review.

Lab: machine-suggested vs source-supported comparison.

Deliverable: documented AI research protocol with failure log.

WEEK 05 · CONTRADICTION

What happens when the sources disagree?

Build the strongest case for competing readings. Preserve date conflicts, witness divergence, institutional interpretation, and missing evidence.

Lab: contradiction graph / provenance network.

Deliverable: structured contradiction set with next-test questions.

WEEK 06 · CODE THE EVIDENCE

What becomes visible when the evidence computes?

Turn the ledger into timelines, maps, networks, matrices, small simulations, sonifications, or other computational views.

Lab: SVG, p5.js, D3.js, or Python/pandas visualization.

Deliverable: one visual view + one equivalent nonvisual representation.

WEEK 07 · THE PUBLIC RECORD

Can someone else inspect your reasoning?

Accessibility audit, source release, interface critique, documentation, limitations, uncertainty statement, and public presentation.

Lab: GitHub Pages publishing + keyboard/screen-reader audit.

Deliverable: final evidence machine.

Assignments

01 · CLAIM LEDGER

One question, many states

Track a claim as supported, contradicted, unresolved, missing, or unverified. Students must record what evidence would change the state.

02 · SOURCE AUTOPSY

Follow one object back

Choose a circulating clip, photograph, quotation, chart, or story and trace it back through copies and interpretations to its best available source.

03 · TWO VIEWS / SAME DATA

Visual + nonvisual

Build a coded visualization and an accessible table/text equivalent from the same structured dataset. Compare what each reveals and hides.

04 · FINAL EVIDENCE MACHINE

A public, inspectable investigation

Publish the source ledger, interface, uncertainty statement, accessibility layer, and methodological notes as one coherent computational work.

Proposed assessment

25%Source and evidence practice
25%Code / data studies
40%Final evidence machine
10%Critique, accessibility, documentation
The project is not evaluated on whether the student’s starting hypothesis was correct. It is evaluated on whether another person can reconstruct how the student reached the published state of the evidence.

Final project requirements

01A precise research question

Not a predetermined answer.

02A machine-readable source ledger

JSON, CSV, or documented equivalent.

03At least one coded evidence view

Timeline, graph, map, matrix, sonification, or custom instrument.

04A contradiction layer

Competing records stay visible.

05An uncertainty statement

What remains unknown and why.

06An accessibility-equivalent representation

Table, transcript, description, keyboard interaction, or other appropriate equivalent.

07Public documentation

Sources, code, limitations, provenance, and update date.

08A human editorial decision

What should be published, withheld, or left unresolved.

Reference case: Omoluabi UAP archive

The existing Omoluabi investigation functions as the reference implementation. It is useful precisely because the records do not collapse into one answer.

EDWARDS AFB / GORDON COOPER

How a story changes across decades

Students can compare archival film inventory, Project Blue Book material, McDonald’s 1968 congressional reconstruction, Cooper’s 1962 public interview, his 1978 letter, and later recollections. The exercise is provenance before belief.

Open media manifest

Accessibility is part of the evidence model

A visualization that communicates only through color, a video without description, audio without transcript, a map without a spatial text equivalent, or a source image without legible transcription creates an evidentiary asymmetry. The course treats that as a research failure rather than a cosmetic defect.

  • Keyboard-first navigation and visible focus.
  • Semantic headings, tables, lists, and native controls.
  • Captions and transcripts for audio/video evidence.
  • Alt text and extended descriptions for meaningful images.
  • Text/table equivalents for charts, diagrams, and maps.
  • Plain-language summaries that do not erase methodological complexity.
  • Source links and identifiers available outside the visualization.
  • Reduced-motion and low-bandwidth compatibility where appropriate.

Editorial boundaries

No belief requirementStudents are not rewarded for proving or debunking an extraordinary claim.
No AI answer as evidenceA model can help locate or organize a source. Its generated statement is not the source.
No invisible contradictionMaterial counter-evidence must remain inspectable.
No false precisionUnknown range, missing metadata, uncertain timestamps, and disputed identities stay marked as uncertain.
